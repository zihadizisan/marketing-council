# Marketing Council Transcript

**Plan:** Tallyhand — Q3 Growth Plan (fictional)
**Date:** 16 September 2026
**Verdict:** REWORK
**Composite:** 2.67 / 10, spread 1 point

> This is a real, unedited run of the `marketing-council` skill, kept as a worked example.
> The plan is invented; the six advisor responses, six peer reviews and chairman synthesis
> are the actual output. Nothing was rewritten to make the council look good — including
> section 3, where the council openly contradicts itself, and section 7, where the chairman
> discards one advisor's entire recommendation.

---

## Step 1 — Intake gate

Nine required components, each assessed PRESENT, VAGUE or MISSING. Four or more MISSING and
the council refuses to convene.

| Component | Status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | PRESENT | 1,000 paying users by 30 September. Specific and dated. |
| Target audience | VAGUE | A demographic bracket, not a market. Contradicts the product's stated focus on freelance designers. |
| Positioning / core message | VAGUE | Built entirely from comparatives. Category-generic. |
| Offer | PRESENT | $12/mo, 14-day no-card trial, $110/yr annual. |
| Channels | PRESENT | Four named channels with per-channel spend. |
| Budget | PRESENT | $40,000, allocated. |
| Timeline | PRESENT | Three phased months with named activity. |
| Success metrics | VAGUE | Named without targets. None connect to the paying-user objective. |
| Owner and capacity | PRESENT | One full-time marketer, one designer at ~50%. |

**Result: 0 MISSING, 3 VAGUE — the council convenes**, with the three gaps passed to every
advisor as explicit unknowns rather than assumptions to be invented.

**Measurement gate: ACTIVE.** Success metrics were VAGUE, which caps the final verdict at
REWORK regardless of how the plan scores on any other dimension.

## Step 2 — The plan as submitted

See [`sample-plan.md`](sample-plan.md) for the full document. Summary of what the council received:

- **Product:** invoicing and expense tracking for freelance designers
- **Traction:** launched February, 180 paying users, ~60% month-three retention, all growth from founder word-of-mouth and one Product Hunt post
- **Goal:** 1,000 paying users by 30 September — a 5.5x increase in 13 weeks
- **Budget:** $40,000 across paid social ($14k), search ($8k), a podcast sponsorship ($10k) and content ($8k)
- **Team:** one full-time marketer, one designer at ~50%

## Step 3 — The six advisors, in parallel

Each advisor received an identical brief and its own lens, with the standing instruction to
lean fully into that lens rather than attempt balance. None saw any other advisor's response.

### Response A — The Creative Director  *(score 2/10)*

SCORE: 2 — This is a competent media buying schedule with no idea in it anywhere, and at $40k against a 5.5x user target in 13 weeks, unremarkable is fatal.
STRONGEST ELEMENT: The podcast sponsorship is the only line item with a chance of producing something memorable — one design podcast, three episodes, a host reading to an audience that actually overlaps with freelance designers. It is a real context, not an impression buy. But nothing in the plan says what the ad would *say*, so even this is only latent.
FATAL FLAW: There is not one thing in this document that would make anyone stop scrolling. "Carousel ads showing the invoice builder" is the creative strategy for a product whose users are professional designers — people whose entire job is judging visual work and who are the most ad-literate audience you could pick. You are showing a screenshot of a form to people who make screenshots of forms for a living. Nobody will screenshot this, forward it, or remember it by Thursday.
KILL CRITERIA: If the designer at 50% is producing carousel variations and blog headers for the whole quarter and nobody is assigned to have an idea, do not run this. The $40k buys distribution for nothing, and you will conclude paid social does not work when what did not work was the ad.
THE FIX: Kill the carousel concept and build the campaign around the thing every freelancer feels and nobody says out loud: the humiliation of chasing payment. Make the unpaid invoice the subject, not the invoice builder.

The braver version, specifically: a public artifact called something like *The Late List* — a running, anonymized tally of how long freelance invoices actually go unpaid, built from real Tallyhand data. Average days overdue by client type. The worst excuse of the month, submitted by users. A live counter of money owed to freelancers using the product. That is a thing designers screenshot and post, a thing the podcast host reads out unprompted because it is genuinely interesting, and a thing that earns links instead of buying them. It also makes the $8k of blog spend stop being two generic posts a week about "pricing" and become the editorial arm of one owned idea.

What it risks: it is off-brand for "trusted partner" — it is funny about something clients do badly, which means some prospects' clients will see it. It requires real data discipline and an anonymization promise you must not break. And it is a bet on one concept instead of four hedged channels, so if the idea lands flat there is no diffuse fallback. That is the trade. Right now the plan has no downside because it has no upside.

The audience line says 25-45 freelancers and small business owners while the product is for freelance designers — that width is why the creative defaulted to generic. You cannot make something sharp for a demographic bracket. And the success metrics measure followers and traffic, which are the metrics you track when you do not expect anyone to care.

### Response B — The Rival  *(score 3/10)*

SCORE: 3 — Nothing here is structurally defensible; the positioning can be copied by Friday, their brand name brand-bid for pennies, and their own metrics won't show them it happened.
STRONGEST ELEMENT: 180 word-of-mouth users concentrated in one niche (designers) — the one asset that cannot be bought. And the $110 annual plan, the only move in this document that takes a customer off the board for twelve months.
FATAL FLAW: The positioning is category boilerplate. "Simpler than enterprise suites, more professional than a spreadsheet" is on four competitors' homepages already and costs nothing to match. A plan whose core claim is free to copy hands away the whole quarter.
KILL CRITERIA: If they cannot name one capability a funded competitor could not ship in 90 days, do not spend $40,000 defending a position that isn't one. As submitted, they cannot.
THE FIX: Pick the designer niche explicitly — the one the product already serves and the targeting abandons — and build the claim on something niche-specific nobody would bother copying (revision-round billing, deposit-then-milestone, scope-creep capture). Move $6k off head-term search into owning that wedge.

Here is what a competitor does the morning this plan leaks.

Week one, $1,500: brand-bid "Tallyhand" and "Tallyhand alternative" and stand up a comparison page. They have no brand-defense line item, so their name goes for $0.40 while they pay $9 for "freelance invoice software." Every dollar of their $22k in paid spend now walks past a competitor's storefront at the bottom of the funnel. Live in 48 hours.

Week two, $2,000: copy the free trial with no card, and publish a free tier for freelancers under a revenue threshold. Against Wave and Invoice Ninja at zero, $12/month with no differentiated claim is not a price, it is a target. The anchor resets and their trial-to-paid drops without their traffic moving.

August, $2,500: they have put 25% of the budget into one podcast, three episodes, non-refundable, unmeasurable. Buy the episodes on either side of their flight with a code, or sponsor the same show's newsletter for a quarter of what they paid. Their $10k becomes the warm-up act.

Late August, free: announce an annual promotion before theirs, because theirs is telegraphed for September.

Total: about $6,000 against their $40,000.

The reason this works is the metrics section. Engagement, followers, traffic, blog rankings — none of those move when a competitor intercepts their conversion. Their dashboard can look healthy all quarter while someone else takes the revenue. They find out in October.

They also concede three positions: free, depth ("gets you paid" — chasing, late fees, payments), and the designer niche their own targeting walks away from.

### Response C — The Brand Strategist  *(score 3/10)*

SCORE: 3 — The positioning is category wallpaper, the audience is a demographic bracket that contradicts the product, and nothing in this $40,000 plan builds an asset Tallyhand still owns on 1 October.
STRONGEST ELEMENT: The podcast sponsorship on a single design podcast is the only line that puts the brand in front of a coherent, self-identifying audience repeatedly in a context they associate with being a designer — that is the one place a distinctive brand association could form, if there were anything distinctive to associate.
FATAL FLAW: "The trusted invoicing partner for modern freelancers" is a sentence any of the forty competitors could paste their logo onto without a single word looking wrong — there is no ownable position, so every dollar rents attention rather than building memory.
KILL CRITERIA: If the founders cannot name, in one sentence without the words trusted, simple, or professional, what Tallyhand is for that a designer would repeat to another designer — do not run this at all, because you will spend $40,000 teaching the market a category, not a brand.
THE FIX: Narrow the position from "modern freelancers" to the audience the product was actually built for — freelance designers — and anchor it to a specific, ownable job ("the invoice that looks like your work"), then make every channel repeat that one line and one look for all thirteen weeks.

The plan contains two different audiences. Background says freelance designers. Target audience says freelancers and small business owners, 25-45, comfortable with software. Those are not the same market, and the second one is not a market — it is a census slice. There is no situation, no trigger, no moment of purchase.

The positioning line is constructed entirely from comparatives: simpler than enterprise, more professional than a spreadsheet. That defines a gap in a category, not a brand. Every invoicing tool since 2011 has claimed that exact middle. It is differentiation logic applied where distinctiveness is needed. Nothing here becomes a phrase a designer repeats, a look recognizable in a feed, or a point of view anyone could disagree with.

The spend is 55% direct-response activation and $8,000 of SEO content on generic freelancing and pricing topics — topics that build the category's mental availability, not Tallyhand's. The blog posts will rank for queries that competitors also answer, and the traffic evaporates when cadence stops. The metrics confirm the orientation: followers, traffic, rankings. Not one of them measures whether more designers know what Tallyhand is for than knew in June.

The real cost is not this quarter. It is that 180 users came from founder word-of-mouth — meaning something about this product is already repeatable in a specific community — and the plan abandons that signal for a generic message aimed at everyone.

### Response D — The Operator  *(score 3/10)*

SCORE: 3 — Strategically this may be fine, but as a production schedule it is unbuildable: 1.5 people are being asked to run four channels simultaneously from a cold start with no asset count, no owner per workstream, and no defined first week.
STRONGEST ELEMENT: The channel-to-month sequencing is at least staged rather than all-at-once — paid social and search in July, podcast in August, scale in September. That's a real shape. And the budget is allocated to specific channels with named tactics, which means someone can actually cut a PO on Monday. The offer is fully specified, so there is nothing to decide before a landing page can be built.
FATAL FLAW: Four channels launching in a 13-week quarter against 1.5 headcount, with no asset inventory. Count what July alone requires: paid social carousels for two platforms (Instagram and LinkedIn are different aspect ratios and different copy registers — call it 3 concepts x 4 frames x 2 platforms = 24 discrete design units minimum), Google Search ad groups with 15-20 ad variants and matching landing pages, plus 8 blog posts in July alone. That is roughly 50+ assets in month one, produced by a designer at 50% — around 10 working days of design capacity for the whole month — and one marketer who is simultaneously the media buyer, copywriter, blog editor, analyst, and podcast negotiator. The marketer is the bottleneck for all four workstreams at once. Something silently drops, and historically it is the blog and the ad refresh, which are precisely the two things the September "scale what's working" step depends on.
KILL CRITERIA: If the designer's 50% is not contractually ring-fenced — if that person is also doing product UI, which at a 180-user February-launch startup they almost certainly are — do not run this. Paid social with $14,000 and no reliable creative pipeline is a money incinerator. The ad creative refresh in August is the tell: one line, no owner, no asset count.
THE FIX: Cut July to one channel — Google Search only, $8,000 — and spend week one building the measurement and landing infrastructure instead of creative.

Week one, concretely: Monday, stand up conversion tracking and define what a "paying user" event is in analytics, because the success metrics section tracks followers and traffic and not a single revenue number, which means in September nobody can answer "what is working"; Tuesday-Wednesday, one landing page plus five search ad variants; Thursday, keyword list and account build; Friday, live. Fourteen-day version: search ads running against one page, with trial-to-paid conversion instrumented, and a baseline CAC number. That number tells you whether 820 new users in 13 weeks is arithmetically possible on $40,000. Everything else waits for that number.

### Response E — The Customer  *(score 3/10)*

SCORE: 3 — Nothing here would make the target buyer stop scrolling, and nothing explains why they should pay $12 a month when a spreadsheet is free and already works.
STRONGEST ELEMENT: The trial. Fourteen days, no card. That's the only part fully understood on first read and the only part where saying yes costs nothing.
FATAL FLAW: The plan assumes the buyer has a problem they are shopping for. They are not. The invoice spreadsheet is fine. Nobody wakes up wanting invoicing software — so ads that show "the invoice builder" are answering a question nobody asked.
KILL CRITERIA: If people in this segment are not actually annoyed by how they invoice today — if they'd shrug and say "it's fine" — then don't run this. That is $40,000 to interrupt people who are content.
THE FIX: Replace "trusted invoicing partner for modern freelancers" with the specific, embarrassing moment the buyer would recognize — chasing a client for money that's 40 days late, or rebuilding the same spreadsheet every January — and lead every ad with that moment instead of the product.

Here is what actually happens. Scrolling Instagram between jobs. A carousel appears showing screens of some software. The name Tallyhand tells you nothing — it sounds like a card game. "Simpler than the enterprise accounting suites" means nothing to someone who has never used an enterprise accounting suite, uses a spreadsheet, and does not feel unprofessional using it. So the whole comparison the message is built on is between two things the buyer has no relationship with.

"Trusted" is the wrong word. Trusted by whom? They have 180 users. They are asking for the part of a business where money comes in, from a company nobody has heard of. Nothing in this plan says who else uses it or what happens if they disappear in a year — that is the real fear, not spreadsheet fatigue.

Then the audience: freelancers and small business owners, 25 to 45, comfortable with software. That is most of the internet. It does not describe when anyone would want this. And if the product is really for freelance designers, why talk to everyone? The podcast is the only place this would be heard about from someone already trusted, and it is three episodes in one month.

There is no answer to what happens on signup, whether existing invoices come across, or how long setup takes. Fourteen free days don't help if it looks like an afternoon of typing.

### Response F — The Performance Marketer  *(score 2/10)*

SCORE: 2 — The plan commits $40,000 against a 5.5x user target with no CPA, no conversion assumptions, and no revenue metric anywhere in its success criteria, and the arithmetic it implies is roughly 4x worse than category norms.
STRONGEST ELEMENT: The offer is priced clearly enough to compute against — $12/month with a 14-day no-card trial gives a real LTV anchor, and the annual plan at $110 is a legitimate payback-compression lever if used deliberately rather than as a September afterthought.
FATAL FLAW: The plan needs roughly 820 net new paying users from $40,000, a blended CPA of $48.80 against an LTV of about $30 — every acquired customer loses money, and no line in the plan acknowledges this.
KILL CRITERIA: If 60% month-three retention is accurate and cannot be moved before spend starts, do not run this at all. At that curve the unit economics cannot be fixed by better creative, better targeting, or better landing pages.
THE FIX: Replace the entire success-metrics section with a weekly CPA-and-payback dashboard, set a hard kill threshold of $35 blended CPA, and spend July on a $6,000 test budget across two channels only — release the remaining $34,000 solely if measured CPA clears that threshold.

Here is the arithmetic the plan never did.

**The target.** 1,000 - 180 = 820 net new. But 60% month-three retention means the existing 180 are also leaking. Roughly 15% monthly churn implies about 70 of the current base gone by 30 September, so the real requirement is closer to 890 gross additions. $40,000 / 890 = **$44.90 CPA**, ignoring that Q3-acquired users churn too. Gross-of-churn, the true number is nearer $48-55.

**The LTV.** $12/month at ~15% monthly churn gives an average customer life of about 6.7 months, so **$80 gross revenue**. Strip payment processing (~3%) and hosting/support, call it $70 contribution. CPA $48 against LTV $70 looks survivable — until you notice payback lands at month 4.1, and 40% of customers are gone by month 3. The median acquired customer never repays their own acquisition cost.

**The funnel.** Google Search at $8,000: "freelance invoice software" is commercially saturated; $4-7 CPC is realistic, call it $5 → 1,600 clicks. At a 3% visit-to-trial rate, 48 trials; at 25% trial-to-paid (generous for no-card trials, which typically convert 8-15%), **12 customers for $8,000 — a $667 CPA.** To hit $45 CPA on search you need an 11% click-to-paid rate. Nobody converts at 11% on cold search.

**Podcast.** $10,000 for three episodes on one design podcast. A 30k-download show at 1% code-redemption is 300 trials, maybe 45 customers, **$222 CPA.** Podcast attribution is also nearly unmeasurable at this budget.

**Content.** 26 blog posts at $8,000 that will not rank inside 13 weeks. That is a Q1 asset booked as a Q3 channel — $8,000 of the $40,000 produces zero Q3 users, pushing CPA on the remaining $32,000 to **$36 per user across 890.**

Success metrics compound this: followers, traffic, and rankings cannot detect a $600 CPA. You will reach 30 September having hit every metric you track and missed the only one that matters.

## Step 6 — Chairman synthesis (the CMO seat)

The chairman received everything: the plan, the intake table, all six advisor responses
de-anonymised, all six peer reviews, all six reallocation answers, and the anti-drift result.

### 0. Intake Gaps

The plan does not specify these, and every judgment below is constrained by their absence:

- **No CPA, conversion rate, or revenue target anywhere.** The success metrics name engagement, follower growth, website traffic, and blog rankings. None of them connect to the paying-user objective. Not one number in the metrics section can detect whether the plan is working.
- **No definition of the target audience that matches the product.** Background says freelance designers; the targeting section says "freelancers and small business owners, 25-45, comfortable with software." The Brand Strategist called that "a census slice," not a market. The plan contains two audiences and never reconciles them.
- **No ownable positioning claim.** "Trusted invoicing partner… simpler than enterprise suites, more professional than a spreadsheet" is constructed entirely from comparatives. The Rival notes it is free to copy; the Brand Strategist notes any of forty competitors could paste their logo on it.
- **No creative concept.** "Carousel ads showing the invoice builder" is the entire stated creative strategy. Nothing says what any ad would say.
- **No asset inventory, no owner per workstream, no defined first week.** The Operator counted roughly 50+ discrete assets required in July alone against ~10 working days of design capacity.
- **No brand-term defense line item.** Identified only by the Rival.
- **No churn assumption, no LTV, no payback period.** 60% month-three retention is stated as background and never used as an input to anything.
- **No onboarding, migration, or activation detail.** The Customer asked what happens on signup, whether existing invoices come across, how long setup takes. The plan is silent.
- **No provenance for the 1,000-user target.** All six advisors accepted it; five of six reviewers flagged that nobody asked where it came from.
- **No line item for the existing 180 users** — the only channel in the document with proven conversion.

### 2. Where the Council Agrees

Four points of independent convergence. Treat these as the highest-confidence findings in this document.

**The audience definition contradicts the product — 5 of 6 advisors, reached separately.** The Creative Director ("you cannot make something sharp for a demographic bracket" — and named this as the *cause* of the generic creative). The Rival (the designer niche is a conceded position). The Brand Strategist ("the plan contains two different audiences… the second one is not a market"). The Customer ("if the product is really for freelance designers, why talk to everyone?"). The Performance Marketer implicitly, via saturated head-term search costs. Five seats with nothing in common found the same defect.

**The success metrics cannot detect failure — 5 of 6 advisors.** The Creative Director: "the metrics you track when you do not expect anyone to care." The Rival: "their dashboard can look healthy all quarter while someone else takes the revenue. They find out in October." The Brand Strategist: none of them measure whether more designers know what Tallyhand is for. The Operator: "not a single revenue number, which means in September nobody can answer 'what is working.'" The Performance Marketer: "you will reach 30 September having hit every metric you track and missed the only one that matters." Five seats, five different framings, one conclusion. This convergence is why the measurement gate is active.

**The podcast is the strongest line item — and the weakest, depending on who you ask.** The Creative Director and Brand Strategist independently named it the single best thing in the plan: a coherent, self-identifying audience in a context they associate with being a designer. Both then qualified it identically — it is only latent, because nothing says what the ad would *say*. (The disagreement about it is in section 3.)

**The 180 word-of-mouth users are the one real asset — named by the Rival and the Brand Strategist explicitly, and not one advisor proposed funding them.** Reviewer 1 caught this: "all six named word-of-mouth as the one real asset and none proposed funding it." That gap became the dominant peer-review finding.

### 3. Where the Council Clashes

**Clash 1: Is the problem the message, or is the message irrelevant?**

The Creative Director (score 2) argues the plan's fatal flaw is that nothing in it would make anyone stop scrolling, and prescribes *The Late List* — a public data artifact built from real Tallyhand invoice data. The Performance Marketer (score 2) argues the target is unreachable at any creative quality: $48 CPA against ~$70 contribution LTV with payback at month 4.1 and 40% of customers gone by month 3.

Both are reasonable. The Creative Director is right that showing a screenshot of a form to people who make screenshots of forms for a living is a self-inflicted wound, and right that the plan currently has no upside at all. The Performance Marketer is right that better creative moves conversion rates within a range, and no achievable point in that range fixes an LTV that is smaller than the payback period. I do not split this. Six reviewers ranked the Performance Marketer first and named the Creative Director's response the biggest blind spot in the room, on exactly this ground — Reviewer 2: "The Late List loses money faster."

But note what the peer reviewers did *not* say: they did not say the Creative Director's diagnosis was wrong. Every one of them said the diagnosis was correct and the prescription was unaffordable. That distinction matters for section 7.

**Clash 2: Is the podcast the best money in the plan or the worst?**

The Creative Director and Brand Strategist both named the $10,000 podcast sponsorship the strongest element — the only repeated exposure to a coherent audience where a distinctive brand association could form. The Performance Marketer priced it at a $222 CPA and called attribution "nearly unmeasurable at this budget." The Rival went further: it is 25% of the budget, non-refundable, spent in one month, and a competitor can buy the episodes on either side with a code for $2,500 and turn the $10k into a warm-up act. Reviewer 4 was the only reviewer who took money out of the podcast rather than content — $8,000 of it.

Both sides are reasonable and this is not resolvable from the document. The podcast is the only place in the plan where brand memory could form; it is also 25% of the budget committed to a single month with no measurement, before any CAC baseline exists. The disagreement is real because the plan gives no read on the show's size or the host's relationship to the audience.

**Clash 3: Cut to one channel, or restructure the whole allocation?**

The Operator says cut July to Google Search only, $8,000, and spend week one on measurement and landing infrastructure — get a CAC number before anything else moves. The Performance Marketer says run a $6,000 test across two channels and release the remaining $34,000 only if measured CPA clears $35. The Rival says move $6k off head-term search into owning the designer wedge. These are three different first moves, and the Operator's and the Performance Marketer's both route through search — the channel the Performance Marketer independently priced at a $667 CPA. The Operator's plan would spend $8,000 learning what the Performance Marketer's arithmetic already predicts.

### 4. Blind Spots Peer Review Caught

Round two produced findings no individual advisor reached. The convergence here is sharper than anything in round one.

**Retention is the lever, not acquisition — 4 of 6 reviewers independently (3, 4, 5, 6).** Every advisor treated 60% month-three retention as an input or a kill criterion; none treated it as the work. Reviewer 5: fixing retention from 60% to 80% roughly doubles LTV and makes the same spend viable. Reviewer 3: fix churn to 8% and LTV roughly doubles, making the same CPA work. Reviewer 6: "180 existing users, ~$30k of leaked LTV, and a founder who can call every one of them is the highest-return work available — and it costs near zero." Reviewer 4 sharpened it further to the cohort nobody mentioned at all: "the 40% who already left." This is the single most valuable thing the second round produced, and it emerged four times from reviewers who could not see each other's work.

**The $12 price went entirely unchallenged — Reviewers 1 and 2, independently.** Reviewer 2 put it most precisely: "F proves LTV is the binding constraint, then fixes CPA instead." A designer-specific tool at $29/month roughly triples LTV and repairs the payback math without acquiring a single additional user. Reviewer 2 also connected it to round one: the Brand Strategist, the Rival and the Customer all independently argued the wedge is a specialist designer product — which is exactly what justifies the price. Three advisors built the case for a price increase and none of them proposed one.

**The objective itself is the defect — 5 of 6 reviewers (2, 3, 4, 5, 6).** All six advisors accepted "1,000 paying users by 30 September" as fixed. Reviewer 4: "It is a vanity number with no revenue attached, and no one asked the founders to replace it." Reviewer 3 posed the specific question nobody asked: would the founders accept 450 users at positive payback? Reviewer 6: "a retention-first quarter reaching 500 users who stay is worth more than 1,000 who leak."

**The existing 180 are a free research panel, not just a channel — Reviewer 4, alone.** Fifteen paid interviews with current and churned users would answer the Brand Strategist's positioning question, the Customer's "is anyone actually annoyed" question, and the Performance Marketer's LTV input, in two weeks for near zero dollars. This is the only proposal in either round that resolves three advisors' fatal flaws with one action.

**On the budget reallocation question, five of six reviewers converged on the same source of funds:** $8,000 out of Content and SEO (Reviewers 1 partially, 2, 3, 5, 6), on the Performance Marketer's finding that 26 blog posts cannot rank inside 13 weeks and produce zero Q3 users. Reviewer 4 dissented and took $8,000 from the podcast instead. Every one of the six put the majority of the recovered money into retention, onboarding, spreadsheet import, or referral to the existing base — and every one funded measurement and brand-term defense. Six independent allocations, one shape.

**Also caught, once each:** July-August seasonality went unscrutinized (Reviewer 1); *The Late List* is a consent and privacy liability sourced from 180 accounts' billing data, waved at rather than costed (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).

### 5. The Brand / Performance Split

**Roughly 75% activation / 25% equity — and the 25% is not really equity.**

$14,000 paid social plus $8,000 search is $22,000 of direct-response activation, 55% of budget. The $8,000 of content is booked as an equity-building asset but the Brand Strategist showed it builds the *category's* mental availability, not Tallyhand's — generic freelancing and pricing topics that competitors also answer, with traffic that evaporates when cadence stops. The Performance Marketer showed it returns zero Q3 users. So it functions as neither: it is not activation and it does not compound into brand. The $10,000 podcast is the only genuine equity spend in the document, and even that is latent, because nothing specifies what it would say.

**The placement is accidental.** A deliberate 75/25 split is a defensible choice for a 13-week revenue quarter. But this plan did not choose it. The evidence: the equity portion is built from comparative positioning, which the Brand Strategist correctly identified as "differentiation logic applied where distinctiveness is needed" — a plan that had chosen to build brand would have chosen something to be known *for*. Meanwhile the activation portion has no CPA, no conversion assumption, and no kill threshold — a plan that had chosen activation would have instrumented it. Neither half was built to its own standard. The split is what fell out of dividing $40,000 across four channels that sounded reasonable, not a position on the equity-versus-activation axis at all.

### 6. The Verdict

**REWORK.**

**The measurement gate is the binding reason and it is not a technicality.** Success metrics were named but untargeted — engagement, follower growth, website traffic, blog rankings — and not one of them connects to the paying-user objective. Per the gate, the verdict is capped at REWORK regardless of how the plan scores elsewhere. Five of six advisors reached this independently without knowing a gate existed, which is the strongest confirmation available that the cap is correct rather than procedural.

I am overriding nothing on the majority verdict, but I am elevating one minority argument above the room's center of gravity: the Performance Marketer's arithmetic is the strongest single objection here, and all six peer reviewers ranked it first without coordination. It establishes that this plan loses money per acquired customer at any creative quality, any targeting sharpness, and any level of operational competence. That converts every other finding from "improvement" to "insufficient." And the peer round found the corollary the Performance Marketer missed: it fixed CPA when LTV was the binding constraint. The rework must start at retention and price, not at channels.

This is not a KILL. The 180 word-of-mouth users in a single niche, the fully-specified offer, and a founder who can personally call every existing customer are real assets, and four reviewers independently identified a path that uses them. The plan is recoverable. The document as submitted is not.

### 7. The Three Changes That Matter Most

**1. Replace the entire success-metrics section with a weekly CPA-and-payback dashboard, and set a hard kill threshold of $35 blended CPA before any money moves.** Release $6,000 as a July test budget across two channels only; release the remaining $34,000 solely if measured CPA clears the threshold. *Raised by the Performance Marketer as its fix, and by the Operator as its concrete week-one Monday task ("define what a 'paying user' event is in analytics"). Ranked first because it is the measurement gate's own requirement and because every other change below is unverifiable without it.*

**2. Move $8,000 out of Content and SEO into retention, onboarding, and the existing base.** Specifically: spreadsheet import, first-invoice-sent activation, dunning and payment-chasing, win-back on the churned cohort, and a referral mechanism into the designer network that produced all 180 users organically. *Raised by Reviewers 2, 3, 5 and 6 independently, on the Performance Marketer's finding that 26 blog posts cannot rank in 13 weeks. Ranked second because reviewers 3, 5 and 6 each showed the same arithmetic: doubling LTV via retention makes the current CPA survivable, where no channel optimization does. Reviewer 4 dissents on the source — take it from the podcast instead — and that dissent is live, not settled.*

**3. Narrow the audience to freelance designers, price the product at that niche, and make one claim that is not built from comparatives.** Drop "freelancers and small business owners, 25-45." Test $29/month against $12. Replace "trusted invoicing partner" with a specific ownable job. *The narrowing was raised independently by the Brand Strategist, the Rival, the Customer and the Creative Director; the price test was raised only in peer review, by Reviewers 1 and 2, who connected it to those four. Ranked third not because it matters least but because it is the change that takes longest to validate — and it should be validated by Reviewer 4's proposal: 15 paid interviews with current and churned users, which answers the positioning question, the Customer's "is anyone actually annoyed" question, and the LTV input at once.*

One thing I am explicitly not carrying forward: the Creative Director's *The Late List*. All six reviewers named it the biggest blind spot in the room. The diagnosis behind it stands — generic creative aimed at the most ad-literate audience available is a real failure, and it survives into change 3. The artifact does not: 180 accounts cannot produce credible aggregate data, 1.5 people cannot build it, and it carries a consent liability the response waved at rather than costed.

### 8. The One Thing to Do Monday

Open a spreadsheet and list every one of the 180 paying users and every customer who has churned since February, with signup date, last-invoice-sent date, and whether they are a designer. You will know by lunch what the real retention curve is, what fraction of the base is the niche, and who to call first. Nothing else in this plan can be priced until that list exists — and it costs nothing but the morning.
